Content Starts in the Audience's Head — My 5-Step Process

By Rapar Rubel (Rubel Miah) · 10 October 2026 · 3 min read

  • content strategy
  • brand psychology
  • audience research
  • Banglish copywriting
  • social media

Short answer

  • Good content starts in the audience's head, not on the brand's page.
  • My process has five steps — Listen, Decode, Frame, Create, Test.
  • The most important step is the first: collect the exact words people use.
  • AI speeds up research and drafting, but the human decision about what the audience really fears and wants stays with me.

Most brands start content with a question about themselves: what should we post this week? I start somewhere else: what is my audience already thinking?

Before I write a line or design a frame, I work out how the audience thinks — the words they use, what they worry about, what makes them stop scrolling and what makes them trust. Then I build the content to fit that mind. Here is the exact process I use across my own ventures.

Step 1 — Listen: what words does the audience actually use?

I read comments, inbox messages, reviews and search terms, and I copy the exact phrases. Not a summary — the real words.

For The AB Wardrobe, a women's kurti label, the most common question was some version of "Apu, price koto? Fabric ki?" That one line told me more than any brand brief: people want price and fabric answered before they ask.

Step 2 — Decode: what is the need under the request?

Every question hides a feeling. I separate what people say from what they mean, what they fear and what they would share.

A property buyer in Dhaka says, "Is this land's paperwork clean?" What they mean is, "I can't afford one wrong move with my family's savings." What they fear is fake deeds, hidden DAP problems and being rushed by a broker.

You can try this yourself with the interactive mind map on my portfolio.

Step 3 — Frame: one angle, one emotion

Once I know the fear and the hope, I choose a single angle. The brand becomes the answer to a need — not a product looking for buyers.

For Proyojon Abashon, that angle was protection, so we created ক্রেতা সুরক্ষা, a calm buyer-education series explaining deeds, DAP and home loans before ever asking for a sale. For Proyojon Vromon, the angle was that travel is a need, not a luxury — ভ্রমণ হোক প্রয়োজন — with real costs up front.

Step 4 — Create: native to the platform and the language

Now I write and design. The rule: make it feel like it belongs where it appears.

  • Banglish captions where customers speak Banglish
  • Bangla explainers for serious topics like property law
  • Product-led photography where the product must sell itself — for CAP ON HEAD, every cap's photo, angle and description is planned for how it will look on social media and on the website
  • Short AI video and carousels when the idea needs motion or steps

Step 5 — Test: keep the winner, make it a system

I publish variations and watch what people do: saves, shares, replies and sales — not just likes. Whatever works becomes a repeatable template, so the next post starts ahead instead of from zero.

Where does AI fit into this process?

AI is my research assistant and first-draft writer. My personal agent, Paloan, helps me gather patterns from many comments quickly and draft several caption variations in Bangla, Banglish or English.

But the core decision — what does this audience really fear, and what will earn their trust? — stays human. That judgement is the difference between content that gets seen and content that gets believed. (I go deeper into the language side in Why I Build AI That Speaks Banglish.)

A simple exercise to try today

Take your last 30 comments or messages. Sort them into four columns: says, means, fears, shares. Your next five posts are hiding in the "fears" column.

Quick answers

What is audience-first content?

Audience-first content is built from what the audience already says, fears and shares, instead of what the brand wants to say. You start by listening to comments, messages and searches, then shape the message to answer their real question.

What are the five steps of Rapar Rubel's content process?

Listen (collect the exact words people use), Decode (find the need, fear and hope underneath), Frame (pick one angle and one emotion), Create (write and design natively for the platform and language), and Test (keep what drives saves, shares, replies and sales).

Does AI replace content strategy?

No. AI is excellent for gathering patterns and drafting variations quickly, but deciding what an audience truly worries about — and what will earn their trust — is still a human judgement.
